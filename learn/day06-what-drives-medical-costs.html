What Drives Medical Costs? An EDA with the Predictions Written First

1,338 people with health insurance: their age, sex, BMI, children, whether they smoke, their region, and what their medical care cost in a year. Days 1–5 gave me the tools. Today I point them at real data, with one rule: write down what I expect before any code runs, and keep every wrong guess.

Why predictions first? Once you've seen a result, it always looks obvious. A written, timestamped guess is the only proof of what I actually expected. Here are mine, exactly as I committed them:

  • H1Charges are right-skewed: the mean is above the median by about 15%.
  • H2Age has the strongest Pearson correlation with charges (stronger than BMI or children).
  • H3For age vs charges, Spearman will be smaller than Pearson, because charges grow linearly with age.
  • H3bBMI vs charges is U-shaped (very low and very high BMI cost more), so Pearson will be near 0 (|r| < 0.1).
  • H3cChildren and charges have no link (|r| < 0.1).
  • H4Smokers are about 4× as common among the most expensive 25% as overall.
  • H5Tukey's 1.5 × IQR rule flags more outliers than |z| > 3, because of masking.
  • H6Men's smoking rate is at least 40 percentage points higher than women's.

Every number on this page is computed live in your browser from the full dataset (Kaggle, "Medical Cost Personal Datasets"). My Python versions were checked against NumPy, SciPy and pandas: every check passed. The scorecard is at the end. Try to predict each answer yourself before you click.

The Mode as a Detective

2. A Mode That Shouldn't Exist

On Day 2 I learned that continuous data has no useful mode. On real data, a mode is a clue.

Charges are stored to four decimal places. Two different people paying exactly the same amount down to a hundredth of a cent is very unlikely, yet my mode() found a repeated value: . So I looked at the rows behind it:

My first guess was "two different people who happen to pay the same". The rows say otherwise: they match in all seven columns, including BMI to two decimals. That's one record entered twice. I kept it, because one row in 1,338 moves the mean by about 0.07% and age–charges Pearson by 0.0007, and I report it under limitations instead of silently changing the raw data.

Lesson: for continuous data the mode is useless as a "centre", but useful as a duplicate detector.

Tukey: outside [Q1 − k·IQR, Q3 + k·IQR] · z-score: |x − mean| ÷ std > k
Masking: the expensive values inflate the mean and, even more, the std. That pushes the z cut-off far out, so the very points that are unusual hide themselves. Tukey's quartiles barely notice the tail, so they aren't fooled. The lower Tukey fence is negative here, and charges can't be negative, so every flagged point is on the expensive side.

The bigger lesson is in the colours. When a rule flags about 1 in 10 people, they're rarely mistakes. Almost all of them are smokers: the data is two groups mixed together, and Tukey assumes one. "Outlier" is a statement about a rule, not a fact about a person.

Day 3 on Real Data

4. Pearson vs Spearman: Straight Line or Same Direction?

Pearson asks "how close to a straight line?" Spearman asks "when x goes up, does y tend to go up?"

Predict first (H3)

For age vs charges, which correlation is bigger?

Pearson r
Spearman ρ
People shown

Spearman = Pearson on ranks. It detects monotonic relationships (always up or always down, any curve), but not U-shapes.
When Spearman ≫ Pearson, the relationship goes one way but isn't a straight line, or a few huge values are pulling Pearson around. Neither number can show a shape: only the scatter plot can. And all of this is observational data, so a correlation here is not a cause.
Exploratory, not pre-registered: splitting by smoking status wasn't one of my hypotheses. I found it while looking, so it's a lead to test properly later, not a result. With that caveat: within non-smokers, age and charges are strongly related (Pearson about 0.63, Spearman about 0.82). Mixing in the smokers' higher band is what drags the overall Pearson down to 0.30. And BMI isn't U-shaped in this data: for smokers, charges jump around BMI 30.

Two ways, one answer: counting in the room, and Bayes with prior P(smoker), likelihoods P(top | smoker) and P(top | non-smoker).
In P(A | B), the denominator is everyone who has B: the word after the bar names the room. Agreement between the two methods proves the formula is consistent; it does not prove the input is right. (Mine agreed perfectly while my Q3 was wrong, see the mistakes below.)

8. Mistakes I Caught

  • Wrong percentile scale. I first computed Q3 with 0.75 where my function expects 0–100, and got $1,242. Both of my Bayes methods still agreed perfectly on the wrong answer. What caught it was a sanity check: the Tukey fences already implied Q3 ≈ $16,640.
  • Wrong room in Bayes. I divided P(top | smoker) by all 1,338 people instead of the 274 smokers. The word after the bar names the room.
  • "Left-skewed". I described a long tail of expensive patients, which is right skew: skew is named after the tail, not the hump.
  • Code inside the loop. Twice I indented new lines under a for, so results printed seven times.

    counts = {}
    for v in values:
        if v in counts:
            counts[v] += 1
        else:
            counts[v] = 1
    return counts

function pSmokerGivenTop(rows, topShare) {
  const cut = percentile(rows.map(r => r.charges), 100 - topShare);
  const top = rows.filter(r => r.charges > cut);           // the room
  const direct = top.filter(r => r.smoker).length / top.length;

10. Limitations and Next Steps

  • Observational data. Smoking is strongly associated with high charges here. This dataset alone can't show that it causes them.
  • No uncertainty yet. Is a 6.1-point gap between men and women more than chance? That needs a chi-square test (Day 10). The correlations need confidence intervals (bootstrap, Day 9).
  • One duplicate row (rows 195 and 581) was kept and reported.
  • Data origin. I haven't verified how this teaching dataset was collected, so none of this describes any real population.
  • Exploratory findings (the smoker split, the BMI-30 jump) weren't pre-registered. They're leads for a future test, not results.

The research link: an eval set is a dataset too. The "most expensive 25%" room is the same idea as the "flagged" room in last week's detector problem, and the same base-rate trap applies.
